That was a complete neural network: 18 numbers, six concepts, two neurons, trained in your browser in the second it took you to scroll past. The rest of this page takes it apart — how the cramming works, when it breaks, and how to run it backwards. If you noticed it refusing to store weather, that's the sparsity section below.

0.One neuron, many meanings

Open a trained language model and look at one neuron. A neuron here is a single number in a single layer: it takes some value on every input, large for a few, zero for most. If networks were built the way we would build them, that number would mean something. One neuron for quotation marks. One for wetness. One for the Golden Gate Bridge.

They aren't built that way. Below are four neurons from GPT-2, each shown with the text that excites it most, taken verbatim from OpenAI's public neuron dataset.[9] Brightness is the measured activation. Try to finish the sentence “this neuron detects ___” for any of them.

Surveillance footage and lobster Newburg. Court citations and graduation parties. Neuron 3013 up in layer 12 spends its time on Kalamazoo, a sumo wrestler's name, and the back half of the word “warhead,” which is either three things or one thing depending on how charitable you're feeling about tokenization. Dig through the layers and most of them look like this. Nothing has malfunctioned. The network is short on space.

Count the room. GPT-2 small carries 768 numbers per layer and a vocabulary of 50,257 tokens. Even if the only thing it ever had to record were which token it just read, that leaves 65 tokens competing for every dimension. Tokens are the easy part. The model also tracks who is speaking, what language this is, whether the tone is sarcastic, whether we are inside a quotation, inside a URL, inside a legal citation. When Anthropic ran dictionary learning over a production model, it came back with 34 million distinct features[3]. Give each one a private dimension and you need a network thousands of times wider than anything that exists.

So they don't. They cram, storing more concepts than they have dimensions and paying for it. The practice has a name, superposition, and a theory precise enough to make predictions[1]. The model at the center of that theory has 18 parameters and trains in about a second, which makes the usual way of meeting it — a PDF of finished figures — a waste of a good experiment.

Every result below runs live on this page instead, and you get the first experiment before any network does. Knowing what a vector is will carry you the whole way; the ⊕ primers cover the rest as it comes up.

1.The packing problem

Strip the problem to its skeleton and you can play it yourself. You have six features to store, six concepts the model cares about, and two dimensions to put them in. Each feature is kept as a direction, an arrow in the flat space below, and an arrow needs length 1 to count as fully stored. The catch is that arrows interact: any two that aren't perpendicular interfere, so reading one picks up a little of the other, and the score charges you for every bit of it.

Two dimensions hold exactly two perpendicular arrows. You have six. Someone has to overlap.

Primer: vectors, directions, and “interference” — the only math the game needs

A vector here is just an arrow from the center: a direction plus a length. Storing a feature “as a vector” means: when this feature is active, push the hidden state in this direction; to read it back, measure how far the state extends along that direction.

That measuring is the dot product: for arrows a and b it returns (length of a) × (length of b) × cos(angle between). Perpendicular arrows give 0 — reading one picks up none of the other. The smaller the angle between two arrows, the more reading one accidentally reads the other. That spillover is all “interference” means.

Deeper: any linear algebra intro; 3Blue1Brown's Essence of Linear Algebra, episodes 1 & 9.

One more rule: the six are not equally valuable. F1 matters most, F6 least, and thicker arrows are worth more. Drag the arrow tips and push the score as low as you can. Then hand the layout to gradient descent, which starts from your arrangement rather than from scratch — whatever it finds, it found by improving on your idea.

A minute of dragging turns up both strategies the problem allows. The first is clean sacrifice: give the two most valuable features perfect perpendicular arrows and throw the other four away. Nothing interferes, and four concepts stop existing. The second is the squeeze: wedge the extra arrows into the gaps so that everyone overlaps slightly, everyone pays a little crosstalk, and nobody is dropped.

Which one wins isn't a matter of taste. It depends on how often the features actually show up, which is the next two sections.

TakeawayRepresenting more features than you have dimensions forces interference. The network's only real choice is who pays it: drop features cleanly, or overlap them and absorb the crosstalk.

2.The toy model

Now hand the same problem to an actual network. This is the toy model of Elhage et al., exactly as published[1]: an autoencoder with a bottleneck, 18 parameters in total.

The input x holds six features, each off most of the time and on with random strength the rest, which here means active 5% of the time. Importance decays from F1 to F6, so mistakes on F1 cost roughly six times what mistakes on F6 cost. The model squeezes all six through two hidden neurons and tries to rebuild what went in.

Primer: how to read x′ = ReLU(WᵀWx + b), and what “training” does — 60 seconds of notation

x is the list of six feature values going in; x′ is the model's attempt to reproduce them. W is a grid of 12 numbers (2 rows × 6 columns); multiplying by W squeezes six numbers down to two, and multiplying by its transpose Wᵀ stretches two back up to six. Column i of W is exactly the arrow you dragged for feature i in the game. b is six learned offsets added at the end.

ReLU is the simplest nonlinearity there is: if a number is negative, replace it with zero; otherwise leave it alone. Remember that — the entire plot of this article turns on it.

Training means: score the model by its reconstruction error (weighted by feature importance), then nudge every number in W and b slightly downhill on that score, thousands of times. You watched this exact process when gradient descent took over your layout in the game — same idea, just applied to more numbers.

Deeper: the model definition in Elhage et al., Toy Models of Superposition, section 2 [1].

Watch it run with the nonlinearity and without. What follows is training right now, in your browser — the arrows are the columns of W, moving under gradient descent as you read this.

The linear model cannot exploit overlap no matter how long it trains, because every bit of crosstalk between two features lands in the output undiminished, every time. Its best available move is the clean sacrifice you tried in the game: keep the top two, drop the rest. That strategy is principal component analysis, though nothing in the model knows it by that name.

Add a ReLU and a bias and the arithmetic changes. The model learns a negative bias, a constant downward shove applied to every output. The murmur that other features' arrows leak into yours gets shoved below zero, and the ReLU deletes everything below zero. Real signals are loud enough to survive the shove and arrive intact. Out of two cheap parts the model has built a noise gate, and that's what makes the cramming affordable.

This is roughly CDMA, the multiple-access scheme in 3G cellphones. Each phone gets a code direction, signals overlap freely on one channel, and the receiver projects onto each code and thresholds away everyone else's crosstalk. Features are the phones, the hidden space is the channel, the ReLU with its negative bias is the receiver. Qualcomm engineers designed that on purpose; gradient descent just falls into it.

TakeawayWithout a nonlinearity, interference is fatal and a model keeps only as many features as it has dimensions. A ReLU with a negative bias deletes small crosstalk instead, and that noise gate is what makes cramming affordable.

3.Sparsity

The noise gate has exactly one weakness: it deletes only small interference. When two overlapping features fire on the same input their crosstalk is large, it clears the threshold, and the output is corrupted. Everything therefore depends on collisions being rare, and collisions are rare precisely when features are sparse.

Sparsity is the ordinary condition of language data. Of those 34 million features Anthropic pulled out of a production model, nearly every one is silent in any given sentence; “Korean text” and “HTTP request” and “academic citation” almost never turn up together. Rarity is what superposition spends, and the slider below controls how much of it the model has.

Same model as before, with the importances flattened so no feature wins on privilege alone. The slider changes one number, how often features are active. Scrub it slowly from left to right. Training never stops, so the model re-solves the problem live against whatever data you hand it.

But the changes aren't gradual. A configuration holds across a wide range of sparsity and then reorganizes within a few percent of slider travel, the way water sits at 1°C and then doesn't. The resemblance isn't decorative: Elhage et al. showed these really are phase transitions, in the physicists' sense[1].

Look at where the scrub ends. Five near-unit arrows splay evenly around the circle, each paying a small and equally shared interference tax. At high sparsity the gate eats nearly all crosstalk, crosstalk becomes nearly free, and the network prices it accordingly. Capacity stopped meaning “two” the moment collisions became rare.

TakeawaySparsity buys capacity. Features that rarely co-occur can share dimensions almost for free, and the number of features a space holds is set by collision frequency, not by dimension count. The regime changes are sharp phase transitions.

4.Feature geometry

Why a pentagon? Nothing in the loss function asks for a regular figure. It minimizes squared error and stops, and a lopsided arrangement of five arrows would satisfy that description too. Add a dimension and the coincidence turns into a pattern. Below is the same model with eight features in three dimensions, shown two ways — the arrows themselves, which you can rotate, and a quantity Elhage et al. call feature dimensionality:

Di = ‖Wi‖² / Σj (Ŵi·Wj)²

Read Di as how much of a dimension feature i gets to itself. A private, untouched axis gives D=1; sharing an axis with one antipodal partner gives exactly ½; a dropped feature gives 0.

Primer: why the clean fractions? — where ½ and 3/8 come from

The numerator ‖Wi‖² measures how strongly feature i is stored. The denominator adds up how much every feature (including i itself) spills onto i's direction. If i has a private axis, the only spill is from itself, and the ratio is 1. If two features share one axis (pointing opposite ways), each one's denominator counts both — so each gets ½ of a dimension.

The general pattern: when n features split m dimensions evenly — every pair equally spread out, as in a regular polytope — each gets exactly m/n. Eight features in three dimensions: 3/8. Five in two: 2/5. The fractions are clean because the geometry is exact, and the geometry is exact because anything uneven leaves easy loss on the table for gradient descent to grab.

Deeper: “feature dimensionality” in Elhage et al., section 5 [1].

The plateaus at clean fractions are the giveaway. What the network is doing is spreading points around a sphere so they sit as far from each other as it can manage, which is the problem J. J. Thomson posed in 1904 about electrons repelling on a sphere[6] and mathematicians have picked at ever since. The known optimal arrangements are the shapes on your screen: antipodal pairs, triangles, tetrahedra, square antiprisms. Given reconstruction error, 18 parameters, and 122 years less time, gradient descent finds them again.[1]

So stop asking what neuron 3 does. The neuron grid is a coordinate system and nothing more. Directions carry the meaning[10], the network arranges those directions by interference-minimizing geometry, and where the neuron axes happen to fall never enters the calculation.

TakeawayFeatures live in geometry, not in neurons. The network spreads feature directions into uniform polytopes, the known solutions to a sphere-packing problem posed in 1904, and the neuron axes are bystanders.

5.Polysemanticity

So: what does one neuron respond to? Five feature arrows splay around a circle, and a neuron is one axis through that circle.

Below is the model from the top of the page — the same network you fired concepts into, trained at high sparsity into the pentagon and then frozen. Each panel shows one hidden neuron's weight on every feature. (It's also the specimen in the margin map, if your screen is wide enough to show one.)

That's the lobster-and-larvae neuron from the top of the page, reproduced in 18 parameters. Store features as a pentagon and every axis has to cut across several of them, so every neuron reads out a weighted mixture of unrelated things. Polysemanticity is what a working compression scheme looks like from the inside — a prediction of the geometry, not a defect in the training. Olah et al. had catalogued polysemantic neurons in vision models years before there was a mechanism to blame[7], and Arora et al. had found the same overlapping-directions structure in word embeddings before that[12].

This is where the obvious interpretability program, catalogue what each neuron does, hit its wall. The program was doomed by construction, because the neuron basis is not the basis the information lives in. Nothing has been lost. The features are all present, fully linear and fully recoverable. They are rotated away from the axes we happened to be reading.

Which leaves an opening. If superposition scrambled the features by a known trick, the trick should run backwards.

TakeawayPolysemanticity is the cost of superposition, mechanically: store n features as a polytope in m<n dimensions, and every neuron axis must cut across many features. Interpreting neurons fails because neurons were never the unit of meaning.

6.Sparse autoencoders

State the inversion problem in full. You are handed the hidden activations h, two numbers per input, the superposed mush, and asked for the original features back. You do not know how many features there are. You do not know where they point. You have no answer key.

The way in is sparsity, the same property that made superposition possible. If the true features are sparse, the right explanation of any single activation needs only a few ingredients, so the sparsest accurate explanation is probably the true one. Hence the sparse autoencoder[2][4]. Learn an overcomplete dictionary of candidate directions, ten here for at most six real features, rebuild each activation out of dictionary atoms, and charge a penalty of strength λ for every atom an explanation uses.

Primer: autoencoders, dictionaries, and why an L1 penalty forces sparsity — the parts list for the lab below

An autoencoder is a network trained to output its own input — pointless, except you constrain the middle, and the constraint forces it to learn structure. The toy model itself was one (constraint: only 2 dimensions). The sparse autoencoder flips the constraint: the middle layer is bigger than the input (10 slots for 2 numbers — an “overcomplete dictionary”), but it's penalized for having many slots active at once.

That penalty, λ × (sum of activations), is the L1 penalty. Why does it force zeros, rather than just shrinking everything a bit? Because L1 charges the same rate for the first unit of activation as for the last — so the cheapest way to pay less is to switch slots off entirely. (A squared penalty would charge almost nothing near zero, so nothing would ever quite reach it.) Crank λ and slots start dying; that's exactly what you'll see in the lab below.

Deeper: Bricken et al., Towards Monosemanticity, “Problem Setup” [2].

Because this is a toy we have the luxury no one has on a real model: we know the true features, so the SAE can be graded rather than admired. The thick colored arrows below are the truth. The pale arrows are the dictionary, training live. Move λ and the dictionary reacts. You also get one instrument the other figures don't have — click any atom to ablate it, forcing it silent, and watch what the reconstruction loses.

Nobody told the SAE there were five features. Nobody told it they formed a pentagon. It saw two-dimensional points and nothing else, and the sparsity penalty alone drove it onto the true directions, because the sparsest accurate description of superposed data is the original sparse features. The idea is older than interpretability: Olshausen and Field got Gabor-like receptive fields out of natural images in 1996 by asking for a sparse code and nothing else[8].

Those failure modes at the ends of the slider are the working tensions of real interpretability, reproduced at 18 parameters. Too little pressure and the dictionary stays entangled; too much and features die or shatter. Picking λ on a real model is guesswork dressed up as methodology, because there's no answer key drawn in thick color to check against; the current workarounds are proxy metrics and architectural fixes like k-sparse autoencoders[5], which is a way of saying nobody has solved it.

Deleting a suspected feature to see what breaks is the basic move of causal interpretability. Here it costs a click and reports the damage on a meter. On a production model the same experiment is somebody's research program.

Does the recipe survive contact with a real network? Below are three atoms from a dictionary trained this way on GPT-2's residual stream, with verbatim records. You couldn't finish the sentence “this neuron detects ___” at the top of the page. Try it again.

TakeawayThe same sparsity that lets networks cram features in is the lever that pulls them back apart. Dictionary learning with a sparsity penalty recovers the true feature directions from the superposed mush — that is the bet underneath all SAE-based interpretability.

7.Interpretability as decompression

Everything on this page happened in six features and two or three dimensions, and the gap to a real model is enormous: 768 dimensions per layer in the smallest GPT-2, tens of millions of features in the largest published dictionary. The toy has still been a faithful guide. Run this same recipe at production scale and the dictionary comes back with an atom for the Golden Gate Bridge, one for code backdoors, one for sycophantic praise — single clean directions pulled out of neurons that individually meant nothing[3].

The field has not settled the open edges. SAEs inherit superposition's tradeoffs rather than abolishing them: recovered features split and merge as the dictionary grows[2], and some structure isn't a direction at all — days of the week and months of the year come out as circles, which no dictionary of directions is going to recover cleanly[11]. You have handled the raw material of that argument already. Feature death and feature shattering were the two ends of your λ slider.

The picture underneath survives the caveats. A neural network is a lossy compression scheme for the world, with too many concepts, too few dimensions, sparsity exploited without mercy, and interference managed by geometry and a noise gate. Its internals look inscrutable for a mundane reason. We were reading them in the wrong basis, staring at the channel and calling it the message. Read that way, interpretability has a one-line definition:

Interpretability is the decompression problem.

And decompression is hard precisely because the compression worked.
